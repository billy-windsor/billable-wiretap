01 / Essential principles

Five rules for
useful visuals.

These five ideas carry most of the practical value in the full research. Use them before worrying about polish, novelty or animation.

01 / Purpose Begin here

Start with the task

Decide what the viewer must compare, trace, explain, monitor, find or remember before selecting a visual form.

Ask: what should someone be able to answer?
02 / Form Match the purpose

Choose the right form

Use bars for comparison, lines for time, flowcharts for process, trees for hierarchy and networks or matrices for connections.

Ask: does this form make the task easier?
03 / Layout Show relationships

Make structure visible

Use alignment, proximity, grouping, whitespace and clear direction to show what belongs together and what happens next.

Ask: can the groups be seen before they are read?
04 / Integrity Represent honestly

Encode honestly

Prefer aligned position and length for important quantities. Avoid misleading scales, decorative 3D and precision the data cannot support.

Ask: does the geometry preserve the information?
05 / Access Protect the meaning

Design for everyone

Do not make essential meaning depend on colour, hover, animation, tiny text or a large screen. Use labels, contrast, shape and order.

Ask: does the message survive common constraints?
02 / Choose by task

What must the
viewer see?

Choose the primary task to see a strong default. These are starting points rather than rigid rules; always test the result against the actual data and audience.

Strong default

Sorted bars

Use aligned bars or dot plots when the viewer needs to compare discrete values accurately. Sort them when the original order is not meaningful.

Use pie or donut charts carefully when precise comparison matters.

03 / Warning signs

Four things
to remove.

Most weak visuals do not need more styling. They need fewer competing signals, clearer relationships and enough context to support a correct interpretation.

×

Too many colours

A large palette destroys hierarchy and is difficult to remember. Keep colours few, stable and meaningful.

×

Equal visual weight

If every node, border and label shouts, the viewer cannot tell what matters first. Mute context and emphasise the focal point.

×

Ambiguous connections

Every line should have a meaning. Reserve arrowheads for real direction and minimise crossings that make tracing difficult.

×

Missing context

Include the units, timeframe, source or assumptions required to understand a number, status or relationship correctly.

Final principle

Clarity is the deliberate arrangement of information.

The goal is not to remove information indiscriminately. It is to make the important structure visible before the viewer is burdened by the rest.
